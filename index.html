Module 1 · Foundations

Forces, moments and flow

Three ideas carry most of this guide. Forces are arrows that add and split. A force applied away from a pivot makes a turning effect, a moment. And a moving fluid, air or water, pushes on anything in its way with a pressure that grows with the square of the speed.

The question

A sail is pushed mostly sideways by the wind, yet the boat goes forwards. How can a sideways push turn into forward motion, and why does the boat lean over while it does?

1.1Forces are arrows: they add, and they split

A has a size and a direction, so it is drawn as an arrow, a . Two forces acting together add tip to tail: the single arrow from the start of the first to the end of the second, the , has exactly the same effect as the pair.

The reverse is just as useful. Any force can be split into two along directions you choose. Sailors split the sail's force into a part along the boat's course, the , and a part across it, the . Only the first moves the boat forwards; the second tries to push it sideways and lean it over. Module 5 does exactly this with real sail forces.

Why it matters

Every sailing force is a vector sum: the true wind and the wind made by the boat's own motion add to make the wind the sails feel (Module 3); the lift and drag of a sail add to make its total force (Module 4); that force splits into drive and heel (Module 5).

On the water

You never see these arrows, but you feel them. A boat that leans hard and barely moves has most of its sail force going sideways; easing the sails or bearing away swings the force forwards.

Misconceptions

  • "Forces only push in the direction of the wind." A sail's force is roughly at right angles to its surface, which can point well forward of the wind direction.
  • "Two 10-unit forces always make 20." Only if they point the same way. At right angles they make about 14; opposite, zero.

1.2Moments: turning effects and balance

A force that does not pass through a pivot point tries to turn things around it. That turning effect is a : force × , the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line of action. It is measured in newton-metres (N·m) or, on boats, often kilogram-metres (kg·m, the weight of 1 kg at 1 m).

A heeled sailing boat is a tug-of-war of moments. The sail force acts high up, the opposing water force on the keel acts low down: together they make a . Weight and buoyancy, acting through points that move apart as the boat leans, make an opposing . Crew sitting out on the windward side add to it. The boat settles at the angle where the two balance.

A couple

two equal, opposite, offset forces

The sail pushes one way high up, the keel pushes back low down. Equal and opposite, they do not move the boat sideways once it is going, but they do turn it: heel.

Lever arm

distance matters as much as force

A crew member twice as far out is worth twice the weight. A sail force that acts lower (a reef) needs less righting moment.

Balance

moments add to zero

Steady heel, steady course and steady speed all mean the same thing: the forces and moments acting on the boat cancel. Modules 5 to 7 are the details.

1.3Moving fluids push: dynamic pressure and the square of speed

Air and water are both . When a fluid flows past an object, it pushes with a pressure that scales with its and the square of its speed. This , ½ρV², sets the size of every sail and keel force: force = dynamic pressure × area × a shape factor (the force coefficient).

The square is the key. Double the wind speed and the force on the sails is four times as large. That is why a breeze that feels only "a bit stronger" can overpower a boat that was fine an hour ago, and why reefing comes so suddenly.

Water is about 840 times denser than air. So a keel moving through water at 6 knots meets far higher pressure than a sail in a 15-knot wind, and a keel a fiftieth of the sail's area can resist the whole sideways push of the sails.

Optional detail: the force formula

F = ½ · ρ · V² · A · C

ρ is the fluid density (kg/m³), V the flow speed (m/s), A a reference area (m²) and C a dimensionless coefficient for the shape and angle (a lift or drag coefficient). With V in knots, multiply by 0.514 to get m/s.

Example: a 15-knot wind is 7.7 m/s; ½ × 1.225 × 7.7² = 36 Pa. On 56 m² of sail with C = 1.3 that is about 2,650 N, the weight of 270 kg. Water at 6 knots (3.09 m/s): ½ × 1,025 × 3.09² = 4,880 Pa, about 130 times more per square metre.

1.4Two fluids, one boat

A sailing boat is the only common vehicle that lives across the boundary between two fluids. Above the water, the sails work in air; below, the keel and rudder work in water. Each set of foils makes a force; the boat moves steadily only when the two forces are equal and opposite. That one idea, a sail force balanced by a keel force, explains how a boat sails across and even into the wind (Modules 5 and 6). An iceboat on runners and a land yacht on wheels use the same trick with a different "keel".

Above: the rig

air · large · slow pressure

Big, soft, adjustable foils in a light fluid. Their force sets how fast the boat can go and how much it heels.

Below: the appendages

water · small · high pressure

Small, rigid foils in a dense fluid. They resist sideways sliding, carry the ballast low and let the boat steer.

In between: the hull

buoyancy · resistance · stability

Holds everything up, makes waves and drag as it moves, and gives the beam that helps resist heel.

What you should now understand

  • Forces add tip to tail, and any force can be split into components: for a boat, drive along the course and heeling force across it.
  • A moment is force × lever arm. Sail and keel forces form a heeling couple; weight, buoyancy and crew form the righting moment.
  • Fluid forces scale with density × speed². Twice the wind means four times the force.
  • Water is about 840 times denser than air, so a small keel can balance a large sail.
On the water

When a gust hits, the sail force rises with the square of the wind speed, the heeling moment rises with it, and the boat leans until the righting moment catches up. Easing the mainsheet, sitting out harder or reefing are all ways of changing one side of that moment balance.

Anatomy of a sailboat

Sailing has its own words for almost everything, and most of them name a part that exists for a reason the later modules explain. Learn the parts once here, then hover any underlined word anywhere in the guide.

The question

The skipper calls "ease the vang, tension the cunningham, and drop the traveller a hand". Which bits of string are those, and what does each one actually move?

A drawing from one side hides half the boat: the keel's thickness, the spreaders' sweep, the shrouds splaying out to the sides. 2.2Four groups that make sense of the names

Hull and appendages

float · grip · steer

The hull floats the boat. Under it, the keel grips the water so the boat does not slide sideways, and carries ballast low; the rudder steers. Module 6 shows both are wings.

Spars and standing rigging

hold the sails up

Mast and boom are the spars. The forestay, backstay and shrouds are wires that hold the mast up; they are set once and left alone (standing). Module 12 shows the loads they carry.

Sails

three corners, three edges

Every triangular sail has a head (top), tack (front bottom) and clew (back bottom), joined by the luff (front edge), leech (back edge) and foot (bottom edge).

Running rigging

adjusted while sailing

Halyards pull sails up; sheets pull them in. The outhaul, cunningham, vang, traveller and backstay change the sail's shape. Module 11 shows what each one does.

2.3Directions belong to the boat and the wind

Two sets of directions are used at once. Boat directions are fixed to the hull: forward towards the , aft towards the , and, looking forward, on the left and on the right. They do not change when you turn round.

Wind directions depend on where the wind is: the side is the one the wind blows onto, the side the one it leaves from. The boom and sails always swing out to leeward. Which side is windward is named by the : on starboard tack the wind comes over the starboard side.

Why not left and right?

no ambiguity

"Left" depends on which way you face; "port" never does. On a boat where people face every direction, that matters.

Why windward and leeward?

the wind is the reference

Almost every sailing decision is relative to the wind: where to sit, which side the sails go, who gives way. Module 14 untangles the overlapping terms.

Port tack, starboard tack

which side the wind comes over

Not the same as turning ("to tack"), and not the sail corner (the "tack"). The same word for three things: see 14.3.

  • Hull floats, keel grips and carries ballast, rudder steers.
  • Standing rigging holds the mast up; running rigging is adjusted while sailing.

Module 3 · The air

True wind and apparent wind

The sails never feel the wind you would measure standing on a buoy. They feel that wind plus the headwind made by the boat's own motion. This "apparent wind" is stronger and further forward than the true wind whenever the boat is moving, and it is the only wind that matters for trim.

The question

A boat bears away from close-hauled to a broad reach in a steady 15-knot breeze. Suddenly everything feels calm and warm, though the wind has not dropped at all. Then she heads back up and it is blowing hard again. What changed?

3.1Three winds: true, boat and apparent

You can only trim to the apparent wind. It is the only wind the sails, the telltales and the crew ever feel; the true wind is something you work out afterwards. Hold on to that and most of this module follows.

The is the air's motion over the water. Ride a bicycle on a still day and you feel a headwind equal to your speed: that is the , always from dead ahead. The wind felt aboard, the , is the vector sum of the two (topic 1.1).

Because the boat wind comes from ahead, adding it always swings the apparent wind forward of the true wind. Sailing towards the wind it also adds speed; sailing away from it, it subtracts. The masthead and every sail show the apparent wind.

Why it happens

Velocities add like forces. Relative to the boat, still air would rush aft at the boat's speed. The real air also moves with the true wind. The sum of the two is what crosses the deck.

On the water

Trim to the apparent wind, which the telltales and windex show. When the boat speeds up, the apparent wind goes forward, so sheets must come in. Bearing away to a run makes the boat feel calmer than the true wind really is: reef before you turn downwind, not after you round up.

Misconceptions

  • "The wind indicator shows the true wind." On a moving boat it shows the apparent wind. Instruments compute the true wind from it and the boat speed.
  • "A boat can't sail faster than the wind." On a reach the apparent wind can be much stronger than the true wind. Foiling boats sail at three to four times the wind speed and iceboats at about five; their apparent wind is then almost dead ahead (Module 16).
Optional detail: the apparent wind formulas

With true wind speed W at true wind angle β (from the bow) and boat speed V:

AWS = √(W² + V² + 2WV cos β)     tan(AWA) = W sin β ÷ (W cos β + V)

Example: W = 12 kn on the beam (β = 90°), V = 6 kn: AWS = √(144 + 36) = 13.4 kn, AWA = atan(12/6) = 63°. Dead downwind (β = 180°): AWS = W − V = 6 kn.

3.2The wind is stronger higher up: gradient and twist

Friction with the water slows the air near the surface, so the wind strengthens with height: the . The boat wind is the same at every height. So higher up, where the true wind is stronger, the apparent wind comes from further aft. The top of the sail sees a different wind from the bottom.

That is one reason sails are made to : the top is set at a wider angle to the boat than the bottom, to meet its own apparent wind at the right angle. Module 11 shows how the vang and mainsheet control twist.

3.3Gusts and lulls move the apparent wind

A is a sudden increase in true wind speed. The boat cannot speed up instantly, so for a few seconds the true wind is a bigger share of the apparent wind, and the apparent wind swings aft. Close-hauled, that feels like a lift: the boat can point higher. In a lull the opposite happens: the apparent wind swings forward and the sails start to luff, as if the wind had headed. As the boat's speed catches up, the apparent wind settles back.

Gust

apparent wind goes aft and builds

Ease or feather to control heel, then trim for the new speed. A "velocity lift" is not a real wind shift: don't tack on it.

Lull

apparent wind goes forward and drops

Bear away a little to keep the sails full and the speed up, or ease the sheets to match the new angle.

Boat speeds up

apparent wind goes forward

Sheet in as the boat accelerates. Fast boats sail with sheets in tight on almost every point of sail for this reason.

What you should now understand

  • Apparent wind = true wind + boat wind (equal to boat speed, from ahead), added as vectors.
  • Moving always swings the apparent wind forward. Upwind it is stronger than the true wind; downwind weaker.
  • Wind strengthens with height, so the apparent wind comes from further aft at the masthead: sails need twist.
  • Gusts swing the apparent wind aft, lulls forward, until the boat's speed catches up.
On the water

Turning from close-hauled to a broad reach in 15 knots of true wind, the apparent wind drops from about 20 knots to about 11, and the dynamic pressure on the sails to about a third. Turning back up brings it all back. The calm on the run was the apparent wind, not the weather.

Module 4 · The sail

How a sail makes force

Except when running before the wind, a sail is not a bag that catches the wind. It is a wing standing on its end. Set at a small angle to the apparent wind, its curved shape turns the flow and makes a force mostly at right angles to the wind: lift. That is what lets a boat sail across and towards the wind.

The question

Sailing upwind, the crew pull the sheet in hard "to get more power", the boat slows down, and the red wool on the back of the sail starts to spin. What went wrong?

4.1A sail is a wing: lift, drag and angle of attack

A meeting a flow at a small turns the flow, and the flow pushes back. That push is split in two: , at right angles to the oncoming flow, and , along it. "Lift" on a sail is not upwards: it points sideways and somewhat forwards.

Lift grows roughly in proportion to the angle of attack, until the flow can no longer follow the curved leeward side and breaks away: the sail , lift falls and drag soars. Sheet too far out instead and the front of the sail, the , is hit from the wrong side and flaps. Between the two is a narrow "groove" where the sail works best. , strands of wool on both sides of the sail near the luff, show which side of the groove you are on.

Why it happens

A curved sail at a small angle bends the air around it. Bending a flow needs a pressure difference: lower pressure on the convex, leeward side, higher on the windward side. That pressure difference over the sail's area is the force. The air speeds up over the leeward side (where the pressure is low) and slows on the windward side, as says it must; and the air leaves the sail turned, as Newton's laws say it must when a force acts. Both descriptions are the same physics.

On the water

Trim (or steer) so that both luff telltales stream. Leeward telltale dancing: sail stalled, ease the sheet or head up. Windward telltale lifting: sail about to luff, sheet in or bear away. The rule for the jib: "steer towards the lifting telltale, or trim away from it".

Misconceptions

  • "Air over the curved side must meet the other half at the back." The equal transit time story is false. Air over the leeward side actually arrives at the leech well before the air on the windward side.
  • "Pull the sheet in for more power." Only up to the stall. Past it, sheeting in adds drag and heel and costs speed.
  • "The wind pushes the sail." On every point of sail except a run, the sail is mostly sucked from the leeward side: the low pressure there contributes more force than the high pressure on the windward side.
Optional detail: lift and drag coefficients

L = ½ ρ V² A · CL     D = ½ ρ V² A · CD

V is the apparent wind speed and A the sail area. For thin, attached sections CL ≈ 2π(α − α₀) per radian, reduced for low aspect ratio by about AR/(AR + 2); α₀ is negative for a cambered sail, so a curved sail still lifts at zero angle of attack. The induced drag, CL² ÷ (π · AR), is the price of making lift with a short, wide foil, and is why tall, narrow rigs point better.

Typical sails peak at CL of about 1.3 to 1.7 just before the stall. Lift ÷ drag of a whole rig is around 4 to 6 upwind, far below an aircraft wing's 20 or more.

4.2Depth: how curved the sail is

The or depth of a sail is the height of its curve as a fraction of the , typically 8% to 15%. Deeper sails make more lift at a given angle and stall later, at the cost of more drag. Flat sails make less force but point higher and heel less. Deep for power in light air and waves, flat for a breeze and flat water: Module 11 shows the controls that change depth while sailing.

Deep (12–16%)

power

More lift, a wider groove, more forgiving to steer. Good in light air and chop, where the boat needs power to keep going.

Flat (6–9%)

pointing · low heel

Less force and less drag, a narrow groove, can sail closer to the wind. Good when overpowered or in flat water.

Draft position

where the deepest point is

Forward (35–40% back from the luff) gives a rounder, more forgiving entry; aft (50%) gives a flatter entry that points higher but stalls more easily.

4.3Two sails work as one

The jib and mainsail are not two independent wings. The mainsail's lift bends the oncoming air ahead of it (), so the jib sees the wind from further aft and can point higher without luffing. The jib in turn bends the air reaching the main's luff, so the main can work at a smaller angle without its luff lifting. The gap between them is called the . Together they make more lift for their combined area than either could alone.

When dead downwind there is no flow along the sails at all: they are simply stalled, and push the boat with drag. That is why running is slow for a boat without a spinnaker, and why fast boats sail downwind at an angle (Module 9).

Upwind and reaching

lift-driven

Flow attached on both sides, telltales streaming, force mostly at right angles to the apparent wind.

Running

drag-driven

Sails at right angles to the wind, flow separated, force along the apparent wind. Sail area, not shape, is what matters.

The "slot effect"

not a venturi

Air in the slot is slowed, not sped up as old books say. The benefit is the way each sail changes the flow the other sees.

What you should now understand

  • A sail is a wing. Its force splits into lift (at right angles to the apparent wind) and drag (along it).
  • Lift rises with angle of attack until the stall; too small an angle and the luff flaps. Telltales show which.
  • Deeper sails give more lift and drag; flatter sails less force, less heel and better pointing.
  • Jib and main help each other. On a run the sails are stalled and push by drag.
On the water

The crew in the question sheeted past the stall: the leeward telltale spinning is the sign. Easing the sheet back until both telltales stream gives more lift, less drag and less heel, and the boat speeds up again.

Module 5 · Sailing forces

Points of sail: drive and heeling force

The direction a boat sails relative to the wind decides almost everything: how the sails are set, how fast it goes, how much it leans. The total sail force splits into a forward part that drives the boat and a sideways part that heels it and pushes it to leeward. Upwind the sideways part wins by far.

The question

Close-hauled in 16 knots of wind the boat heels 15° and does 6.8 knots. Bear away onto a beam reach, ease the sheets, and she leans less and speeds up to 8 knots, although the wind on the sails is now only slightly weaker. Where did the heeling force go?

5.1The points of sail

The name the boat's heading relative to the true wind. Within about 40° either side of the wind is the : the sails cannot be set at a useful angle and simply flap. Just outside it the boat is , sails pulled in tight. Turning further away, she passes through a , a (wind on the side) and a , to a with the wind from astern. Turning away from the wind is ; turning towards it is heading up.

On every point of sail the sails are set at roughly the same angle of attack to the apparent wind (Module 4), so they are pulled in tight upwind and let out further and further as the boat bears away.

Why it happens

The sail's total force is mostly lift, at right angles to the apparent wind. Close-hauled the apparent wind comes from about 25–30° off the bow, so a force at right angles to it points mostly sideways. As the boat bears away the apparent wind moves aft, and the force swings round to point forward.

On the water

Every change of heading needs a change of trim: ease the sheets as you bear away, pull them in as you head up. If the boat is overpowered on a beat, bearing away a few degrees (with sheets eased) reduces heel at once; so does easing the main.

Misconceptions

  • "Running with the wind is fastest." For most boats a broad reach or beam reach is faster. On a run the apparent wind is weakest and the sails can only push.
  • "Upwind the wind pushes the boat backwards." The sails' total force still points slightly forward of abeam. That small forward share, against a slippery hull, is enough.

5.2Port tack and starboard tack

Every point of sail exists on two . On starboard tack the wind comes over the starboard side and the boom is out to port; on port tack it is the other way round. The boat's physics is the same on both; what changes is which side everyone sits on, and, under the collision regulations, who must keep clear: a port-tack boat gives way to a starboard-tack boat.

Close-hauled

≈ 40–45° true · sheets in hard

Maximum heeling force, modest speed. The only way to go upwind is a zig-zag of close-hauled legs (Module 9).

Beam reach

≈ 90° true · sheets half out

Apparent wind well forward of the beam, force mostly forward, little heel. Often the fastest and most comfortable point.

Run

≈ 180° true · sheets right out

Sails stalled and pushing. Little heel, but the boat rolls, and the boom can swing across violently in a gybe (Module 10).

What you should now understand

  • Points of sail run from the no-go zone through close-hauled, reaching and running.
  • Total sail force = driving force (forward) + heeling force (sideways). Upwind the heeling force is several times the drive.
  • Bearing away swings the force forward: less heel, more speed, up to a reach.
  • Sails are let out as the boat bears away, to keep the same angle to the apparent wind.
On the water

The heeling force in the question did not vanish; it turned into drive. On a beam reach the apparent wind is at about 60° and the sail force points well forward, so more of the same force moves the boat and less of it leans her over. Module 6 · Under the water

Keel and rudder: the underwater wings

Upwind, the sails push the boat sideways about three times harder than they push it forwards. Something must push back. The keel does, and it does it the same way the sail does: as a wing, meeting the water at a small angle. The rudder is a smaller wing again, and the balance between sail and keel decides how the boat steers.

The question

Straight after a tack, a boat that points the bow back up to close-hauled before regaining speed seems to slide sideways and won't go. Bear away for a few seconds, build speed, and she grips and climbs to windward. Why does the keel need speed to work?

6.1Leeway: the keel works because the boat slips

A keel only makes sideways force if the water meets it at an angle. So a boat sailing upwind does not travel exactly where her bow points: she crabs slightly sideways, at a angle of typically 3–6°. That angle is the keel's angle of attack. Its lift, at right angles to the flow, points to windward and balances the sails' heeling force. The rudder and the hull itself add some too.

Lift goes with speed squared (Module 1), so the angle needed falls fast as the boat speeds up. Halve the speed and the keel needs four times the leeway for the same force; at very low speed it would need more than it can give, stalls, and the boat slides sideways. Making lift also costs , which is why keels are deep and narrow: a high makes lift more cheaply.

Why it happens

The keel is a symmetrical foil. Pointed straight along the flow it makes no lift. Only the small angle created by the boat crabbing sideways turns the water and makes the force. Because water is about 840 times denser than air, a keel of 1–2 m² can balance 50–60 m² of sail.

On the water

"Speed first, then height": after a tack or in waves, sail slightly free to build speed before pointing high. Keep the boat from heeling too far, which tilts the keel and makes it less effective. Raising the centreboard on a dinghy downwind removes drag where no side force is needed.

Misconceptions

  • "The keel is just ballast." On a keelboat it is ballast and a wing. On a dinghy the centreboard is only a wing; it has no ballast at all.
  • "A good keel has no leeway." With no leeway there is no lift. The skill is in making the needed lift at the smallest angle, with the least drag.

6.2Kinds of keel

Every keel does the same two jobs: it is a wing that resists sideways slipping, and on a keelboat it carries the ballast. The shapes differ in how they trade depth, grip and drag. Module 7 · Staying upright

Heeling and stability

The sail force acts high up and the keel force low down, so together they lean the boat over. The boat leans until its weight and buoyancy, now acting through points that have moved apart, push back just as hard. How far it leans, and whether it comes back from a knockdown, depends on its beam, the height of its weight and the shape of its whole hull.

The question

In a building breeze the boat is heeling 25°, the helm is heavy and speed is falling. One crew says "everyone on the rail", another "flatten the main", the skipper says "put a reef in". Which of these reduces heel, by how much, and why?

7.1The heeling moment and the righting moment

The sails' and the keel's equal and opposite force make a (topic 1.2): the , side force × the height between the centre of effort and the centre of lateral resistance. As the boat leans, the rig tilts out of the wind and the heeling moment falls away.

Pushing back is the . All the weight acts down through the , held low by the lead keel. All the buoyancy acts up through the , the centre of the underwater volume. When the hull heels, the leeward side goes deeper and B moves out to leeward. The horizontal gap between the two lines of force is the ; displacement × GZ is the righting moment. The boat settles at the angle where the two moments are equal.

The view from astern shows B moving out to leeward, but the reason is easier to see in 3D. As she heels, the leeward side of the hull digs in and the windward side lifts out, so the shape that is under water, and the waterplane where the hull cuts the surface, both shift to leeward. B is the centre of that underwater shape, so it moves with them. G is fixed in the boat, held low by the lead.

Why it happens

Heel makes the righting moment and reduces the heeling moment at the same time, so there is always a balance angle until the sail force exceeds the largest righting moment the hull can give. Wind force grows with speed squared, so each extra knot of wind costs more heel than the one before.

Module 8 · Speed

Hull speed and resistance

However hard the wind blows, a heavy keelboat seems to hit a speed it cannot pass. The limit is not the sails but the hull: it makes waves as it moves, and at a certain speed it is trapped between its own bow wave and stern wave. Light boats can escape by planing over the top.

The question

As the breeze builds, a 10 m cruiser on a reach goes from 6 to 7 knots easily. Then she seems stuck near 8: doubling the drive from the sails adds less than one knot. Why does each extra knot cost so much more than the last?

8.1Where the drag comes from

Water resists a moving hull in three main ways. is the water rubbing along the ; it grows roughly with speed squared and dominates at low speed. is the energy carried away by the waves the hull makes; tiny at low speed, it rises very steeply as the boat approaches hull speed. from the keel making side force (Module 6), plus extra drag from heel and the rudder, add on top when sailing upwind.

Why it happens

A hull pushes water aside at the bow and lets it back at the stern, making a wave that travels with the boat at boat speed. Waves of a given speed have a fixed length: 2πV²/g, so twice the speed makes a wave four times as long. At about 1.34 × √(waterline length in feet) knots the wave is as long as the hull. Faster still, the stern drops into the trough behind the bow wave and the boat has to climb its own bow wave: enormous extra drag.

On the water

Near hull speed, extra power mostly makes waves and heel. Reefing costs little speed but brings big gains in comfort and control. Long waterline length is the cheapest speed there is, which is why designs stretch the waterline with plumb bows and wide sterns.

Misconceptions

  • "Hull speed is a hard limit." It is the start of a steep wall, not a barrier. Heavy boats can exceed it a little; surfing down waves exceeds it for a while; light boats plane far beyond it.
  • "A clean hull doesn't matter much." At cruising speeds friction is half or more of the drag. Weed and barnacles can double it.
Optional detail: hull speed and the Froude number

Wavelength λ = 2πV²/g     Fn = V / √(g · LWL)

Setting λ = LWL gives V = √(g · LWL / 2π), that is Fn = 0.40. In knots with LWL in feet this is the familiar 1.34 √LWL; in metres, 2.43 √LWL. The reference boat's 8.8 m waterline gives 7.2 knots.

The is speed scaled to size: boats of any length make the same wave pattern at the same Fn. Displacement sailing hulls rarely exceed Fn 0.45–0.5; planing begins around 0.5–0.6 and fast dinghies and foilers reach 1.5 and more.

Module 9 · Performance

Polars, VMG and going upwind

Put everything so far together, wind, sails, keel, heel and hull, and you can predict how fast the boat sails at every angle to the wind. That prediction, drawn as a polar diagram, answers the practical questions: how close to the wind to sail, when to tack, and why the fastest way to a mark directly downwind is often not straight at it.

The question

The windward mark is 2 nautical miles dead upwind. One helm pinches as close to the wind as the boat will go; another sails 10° lower, a knot faster, and has to sail further. Who gets there first?

9.1The polar diagram

9.2VMG: speed towards where you want to go

Upwind, what matters is not boat speed but : the part of the speed directly towards the wind. Sail too close and the boat slows sharply; sail too free and most of the speed goes sideways. Close to the wind the apparent wind angle is too small for the sails to make much drive while the keel still has to resist the full side force, so speed collapses. Further off the wind the boat goes faster but cos(angle) falls. VMG = speed × cos(angle) peaks in between.

  • "Point as high as you can." Pinching keeps the bow pointed at the mark but the boat slows and slides sideways; VMG drops.
  • "Downwind, sail straight at the mark." Faster boats, and most boats in light air, reach the leeward mark sooner by sailing broad reaches and gybing (). Foilers and iceboats gybe downwind faster than the wind itself (Module 16).

Why it happens

Broadly, the rules ask the boat that can more easily get out of the way to do so. A port-tack boat close-hauled can bear away behind a starboard-tacker without losing much; the windward boat of two on the same tack can head up or bear away, while the leeward boat can only head up into the wind.   • "Sail always has right of way over power." And a sailing boat with her engine running is a power-driven vessel.
  • "Right of way means I can hold my course regardless." Every vessel must avoid a collision. A stand-on boat that sees the other isn't keeping clear must act.

Module 10 · Manoeuvres

Steering, tacking and gybing

A sailing boat changes from one tack to the other in one of two ways: by turning its bow through the wind (a tack) or its stern (a gybe). Both are routine, but they behave very differently, and the difference comes straight from the apparent wind and the forces on the sails.

The question

Tacking, the boom drifts gently across and the boat slows down. Gybing in the same wind, the boom can slam across the cockpit hard enough to break gear or injure someone, yet the boat hardly slows. Why are the two manoeuvres so different?

10.1The rudder needs flow

The is a small wing at the stern. Turned a few degrees, the water flowing past it makes a sideways force that swings the stern one way and so the bow the other. With a , push it to port and the bow turns to starboard; a turns the way the bow goes. Like the keel, the rudder only works with water flowing past: a stopped boat has no , and a rudder turned too far (beyond about 25–35°) stalls and acts as a brake.

Steering with the sails

Sheeting in the main pushes the stern away and the bow towards the wind; easing it and backing the jib does the opposite (Module 6). Good helms steer with trim as much as with the rudder.

Rudder stall

Past its stall the rudder makes drag, not turning force. Heeled and overpowered, it can lose its grip altogether: a .

At high speed or heel, air can be drawn down the low-pressure side of the rudder from the surface (), and grip vanishes suddenly.

10.2Tacking and gybing

To , the boat turns towards the wind, through the no-go zone with the sails flapping, and bears away on the other side. During those seconds the sails make no drive, so the boat slows; turn too slowly and she stops head to wind, . But the apparent wind is from ahead, the sails are almost unloaded as they cross, and the boom moves gently.

To , the boat turns away from the wind until the wind crosses the stern. The sails stay full and driving until the last moment, then the wind gets behind the mainsail and throws it across the boat, from fully out on one side to fully out on the other. Unless the crew first pull the boom in to the centreline, it swings through 150° or more and stops with a crash. An , when a helm running downwind lets the boat sail , is one of the commonest causes of injury on sailing boats.

Why it happens

In a tack the apparent wind swings through the bow, where it is strongest and the sails are edge-on to it. In a gybe the apparent wind swings through the stern, where it is weakest, but the mainsail is broadside to it and fully loaded. The sail does not lose its force as it crosses; it reverses it.

On the water

Tack positively: build speed, turn smoothly and not too slowly, and let the jib across as the bow passes the wind. Gybe under control: warn the crew, sheet the main in to the centreline, turn, ease out on the new side. Running in a seaway, rig a from the boom end forward to stop an accidental gybe.

Misconceptions

  • "Gybing is safer because the boat stays upright." Heel stays low, but the loads in the boom, mainsheet and rig peak, and anyone in the boom's path is at risk.
  • "Tacking is the risky one because you might stop." Stopping in irons is embarrassing, not dangerous: back the jib, and the bow falls off.

10.3Stopping, and losing control

a parked sailing boat

Heel and weather helm overpower the rudder, usually in a gust or a wave on a reach or run, and the boat spins head to wind laid on her side. Ease the main (vang and sheet) early.

Downwind in a breeze, the rig can drive the boat into growing rolls until it gybes or broaches. Sheet the main in a little, tighten the vang, and steer under the rolls.

Module 11 · Trim

Sail shape and trim controls

A sail is not a fixed wing. Its angle to the wind, its depth, where that depth sits and how much it twists from bottom to top can all be changed while sailing, each with its own piece of string. Trimming is choosing those four things for the wind and waves.

The question

A gust hits on a close reach and the boat heels hard. Easing the mainsheet lets the whole boom swing out and the sail flaps; easing the vang instead lets just the top of the sail open, and the boat stands up and keeps driving. What is the difference?

11.1Four things to control

Angle

sheet and traveller

The sail's angle to the apparent wind: its angle of attack (Module 4). Set by how far the boom or clew is let out.

Depth

outhaul · backstay · halyard

How curved the sail is (). Deep for power, flat to depower and point.

Draft position

cunningham · halyard

Where the deepest point is. Forward is forgiving and powerful; aft points higher but stalls easily. Wind pressure tends to blow it aft as the breeze builds.

Twist

sheet · vang

How much more open the top is than the bottom. Matches the wind gradient (Module 3) and is the main depowering tool: a twisted top spills wind high up, where it heels the boat most.

A sail is a curved surface, so its shape is easiest to read the way sailmakers do: in sections. Coloured draft stripes are sewn across real sails at about 20, 50 and 80% of the height so the trimmer can see each section's depth and draft position from the cockpit. Seen from above, the stripes show all four controls at once: the angle of each chord to the centreline, how deep each curve is, where its deepest point sits, and how much the upper chords are turned further out than the lower ones (twist).

11.2What each control does

The pulls the boom in and down; pulling down tensions the leech and closes the twist. The moves the boom sideways without changing leech tension. The holds the boom down when the sheet is eased, so it controls twist off the wind. The sets the depth of the lower sail; the pulls the draft forward; the bends the mast, which pulls cloth out of the middle of the mainsail (flatter), opens the top and tightens the forestay.

Why it works

Each section of the sail works at its own angle of attack to its own apparent wind. Twist changes the angle high up, where the heeling arm is longest; depth changes how much lift each section makes. So twist and depth together decide how much force the sail makes and how high up it acts.

On the water

Light air: full sails, a little twist, draft forward, sheets eased slightly so the flow stays attached. Medium: maximum power, top telltales just streaming. Heavy: flat (outhaul, cunningham, backstay on), twist open with the sheet or vang, traveller down, then reef.

Misconceptions

  • "The mainsheet just sets the angle." Upwind it mostly sets leech tension and twist; the traveller sets the angle.
  • "A flat sail is always faster." Only when overpowered or in flat water. In light air and chop, flat sails starve the boat of power.

Off the wind the jib and main are small for the job: the apparent wind is weak (Module 3), and the main blankets the jib. So boats add a , a big, light, deep sail, often larger than the main and jib together, flown loose from the masthead in front of the forestay. It works less like a wing and more like a parachute on a run, and like a very deep wing on a reach.

A symmetric spinnaker is set on a that holds its windward corner out from the mast. That corner is the tack, held by the , a line through the end of the pole; the leeward corner is the clew, on the sheet. The pole's height is set by the above it and a downhaul below. An asymmetric spinnaker, or , has its tack fixed at the bow or on a bowsprit and needs no pole; it is happiest on a broad reach, and boats carrying one sail downwind in a series of broad reaches and gybes rather than running straight (downwind VMG, Module 9).

The pole

square to the apparent wind

Pull the pole aft as you bear away, so it stays about at right angles to the apparent wind. Set its height so the two lower corners are level.

The sheet

ease to the curl

Ease the sheet until the luff just starts to curl, then trim a touch. A spinnaker on the edge of curling is making the most force. If it collapses, trim hard, or bear away.

Up and down

in the lee of the main

Hoist and drop it behind the mainsail, bearing away first, where the main blankets it and it can't fill until the crew is ready. A sail that fills half-hoisted can pull the boat over.

Broaching

a big sail high up

On a reach the spinnaker's force is high and to leeward: in a gust the boat heels, weather helm grows and the rudder lets go (Modules 6 and 10). Ease the spinnaker sheet and the vang early, and bear away with the gust.

Rolling on a run

the death roll

Pole too far aft and sheet too far out, the sail swings from side to side and rocks the boat. Trim the sheet in, bring the pole forward a little and lower it.

Gybing

pole across, or round the front

With a symmetric spinnaker the pole moves to the new windward side and the guy and sheet swap jobs. A gennaker gybes like a jib: ease the old sheet, the clew goes round in front of the forestay, trim the new one.

Module 12 · Structure

Rig loads: mast, stays and sheets

Every newton the sails push sideways has to get from the sailcloth into the hull and the keel. It goes through the sheets and the mast, and down the shrouds and stays. Their size is set by one number above all: how much righting moment the boat can produce.

The question

A 10 m yacht weighs 5 tonnes, yet its windward cap shroud, a wire the thickness of a pencil, can carry a load equal to the weight of two or three family cars, and the mast pushes down on the hull with a force approaching the whole boat's weight. Where do loads that large come from?

12.1How the mast stays up

The mast is a column. The sails pull its top sideways and forwards; the , and pull it back down to the hull at the . Those wires are in ; the mast between them is squeezed in by the sum of all their downward pulls, plus the halyards.

When the boat heels, the heeling moment from the sails is passed through the windward shrouds into the hull, where the keel's righting moment balances it. The shroud load is roughly that moment divided by the sideways distance from the mast to the chainplate. So the stiffer the boat (the more righting moment), the higher the rig loads; and the closer the chainplates are to the mast, the higher still. Designers size the rig on the boat's righting moment at about 30° of heel, with a of 2.5 to 3 on the wires' .

Why it happens

Moment balance (topic 1.2) again: a large moment carried over a short lever arm makes a large force. A chainplate 1.45 m from the mast has a lever a tenth as long as the mast is tall, so the shroud must pull about ten times harder than the sails push at their centre of effort.

On the water

Set so the leeward shrouds just stay taut in a breeze, keeping the mast straight sideways (). A slack leeward shroud lets the mast flex and can let it . Inspect swages, terminals, pins and chainplates every season: rigs rarely fail in the middle of a wire.

Misconceptions

  • "The mast holds the sails up, so it carries the sail force." The mast mainly carries compression. The sideways load is turned into tension in the shrouds.
  • "Wind strength sets rig loads." Once the boat is heeled, the sails cannot push harder than the hull can resist: the maximum righting moment sets the maximum rig loads.

12.2Sheet loads and winches

A sheet holds the clew against the sail's whole pull, which grows with the square of the apparent wind speed and with sail area. A multiplies the crew's effort: the handle is a lever, and gears inside give a "power ratio" roughly equal to the winch's size number (a size-40 winch gives about 40:1). A tackle gives in the same way: a 4:1 mainsheet needs a quarter of the pull, through four times the length of rope.

Wire and rod

stiff · low stretch

Stainless steel 1×19 wire or solid rod for the standing rigging. Low stretch keeps the mast in column; fatigue and corrosion at terminals are the usual failures.

Fibre ropes

high-modulus cores

Polyester for sheets that need some give; Dyneema and similar fibres for halyards and even shrouds, as strong as steel for a fraction of the weight, with almost no stretch.

Fittings

the weakest link

Shackles, blocks, clutches and chainplates have their own working loads. A system is only as strong as its weakest part: check every item in the load path.

Module 13 · The real world

Wind shifts, waves and tide

Real wind is never steady: it shifts in direction and pulses in strength. The water moves too, carried by tidal streams, and wind blowing against a stream builds steep, breaking seas. Sailing well means using the shifts, allowing for the current and choosing when to go.

The question

Two identical boats leave the start of a beat together, one on each tack. Ten minutes later one is a quarter of a mile ahead, though neither did anything better. Then, crossing a channel to a harbour entrance, both point straight at it and end up well down-tide of it. What happened, twice?

13.1Lifts and headers

When the wind direction changes, a close-hauled boat must change its heading to keep the same angle to the wind. If the shift lets her point closer to where she wants to go, it is a ; if it forces her further away, a . The same shift is always a lift on one tack and a header on the other. A wind that (shifts clockwise) lifts a boat on starboard tack and heads one on port tack; a wind that does the reverse.

Why it happens

A close-hauled boat is tied to the wind, not to the compass. Turn the wind and both tacks turn with it, one towards the mark and one away.

On the water

"Tack on the headers": when headed, tack onto the lifted tack. Watch the compass: on each tack, note the average close-hauled heading; a heading worse than average is a header. Look upwind for dark patches of gusts and for how other boats are pointing.

Misconceptions

  • "A lift means the wind got stronger." It is a change of direction. A gust can feel like a lift (Module 3), but that is the apparent wind, and it settles back.
  • "Shifts even out over a beat." Only if you ignore them. Sailing the lifted tack each time banks the gain.

Module 15 · Seamanship

Stopping, mooring and recovery

A sailing boat has no brakes. Every close-quarters job, picking up a mooring, coming alongside, getting a person back aboard, ends with the boat stopped in exactly the right place, and the only brake is the wind on the bow. This module is about judging that stop, and about the shallow water that waits for anyone who gets it wrong.

The question

A dinghy sailor and a keelboat skipper each turn head to wind three boat lengths short of a mooring buoy, at the same speed, in the same breeze. One stops a long way short; the other sails straight past it. Which is which, and why?

15.1Stopping: head to wind, and the glide

A sailing boat can't sail straight into the wind (Module 5), but that is also how it stops. Turn and : the sails flap edge-on, make no drive, and the wind on the flogging sails, rig and crew pushes the boat backwards. The boat does not stop at once. It , gliding on its own momentum until the water's resistance and the wind's push use it up.

How far it glides depends on two things: how much momentum it has (mass × speed) and how hard it is braked (hull resistance plus wind drag). A keelboat weighs about 25 times as much as a dinghy and its crew, but has only about 4 times the wetted surface and 3 times the windage, so it glides several times further. And because hull resistance falls with the square of speed (Module 8), the last knot is the slowest to lose.

Why it happens

At 3 knots the keelboat's hull resistance is only about 100 N (Module 8), and 12 knots of wind on its flapping sails adds rather more: about 250 N to stop 5½ tonnes, a deceleration of under 0.05 m/s². The dinghy's brakes are about a quarter as strong, but they act on a twenty-fifth of the mass. Halving the speed quarters the resistance, so each knot lost takes longer than the one before.

On the water

Always finish an approach head to wind, so you can stop by letting fly. Approach on a , which lets you speed up (trim in) or slow down (ease) on the way in. Learn your boat's glide on a buoy in open water before you need it beside a dock. If you are short, trim the sails in for a moment to creep up; if you are fast, don't grab: bear away and go round again.

Misconceptions

  • "Come in fast so you have control." You need enough speed for the rudder to work (, Module 10), and not much more: double the speed and the glide more than doubles.
  • "Turning into the wind stops the boat." It removes the drive. A heavy boat then carries her way for tens of metres, and once stopped she begins to drift backwards and her bow falls off the wind.

Module 16 · Going further

Faster than the wind

A boat moving fast makes its own headwind, and that headwind can drive it faster still. This self-reinforcing loop of apparent wind is why iceboats sail at about five times the wind speed, and why foiling boats beat the wind upwind as well as downwind. It is also why the loop has a limit, and why a keelboat never gets near it.

The question

In 12 knots of wind, an America's Cup foiler crosses the course at over 40 knots, and it comes downwind faster than the wind is blowing. Its sails stay pulled in tight the whole time, and the crew feel a gale from almost dead ahead. Where does the extra speed come from, and why can't a cruising yacht do the same?

16.1The loop: speed makes its own wind

Module 3 showed that the sails feel the : the true wind plus a headwind equal to the boat's own speed. Module 4 showed that a sail is a wing, whose force grows with the square of that wind. Put the two together and you get a loop. The faster the boat goes, the stronger the wind it feels; the stronger the wind, the bigger the force; the bigger the force, the faster the boat goes.

Nothing is created from nothing. All the energy still comes from the true wind; the boat's speed only changes the angle and the strength of the wind that the sails can use. But the loop has a brake built in. The headwind the boat makes always comes from dead ahead, so as the boat speeds up, the apparent wind does not only grow: it swings forward. The sail's force, roughly at right angles to the apparent wind, swings round with it from pointing forward to pointing sideways.

Remember that you can only trim to the apparent wind (Module 3). As speed builds and that wind swings forward, the sheets keep coming in, until a foiler heading downwind is sheeted as tight as a keelboat close-hauled.

1 · Speed makes wind

apparent wind grows

The boat's headwind adds to the true wind. At 40 knots a foiler makes more wind than the weather does.

2 · Wind makes force

force ∝ apparent wind²

Twice the apparent wind gives four times the force, so the sails can pull much harder than in the true wind alone.

3 · Force makes speed

while drive > drag

As long as the forward part of the force beats the drag, the boat accelerates, and the loop goes round again.

4 · The brake

the wind swings ahead

Each lap swings the apparent wind further forward and turns the force further sideways. The loop stops when the forward part only just matches the drag.

Why it happens

Speed changes the wind the sails work in. A sail's force depends on the apparent wind squared, and the apparent wind depends on the boat's speed. So speed feeds back into force. The same feedback turns the apparent wind ahead, which turns the force sideways, so each extra knot gives less extra drive than the last, until it gives none.

On the water

Fast boats are sailed by the apparent wind: as the boat accelerates, sheet in; if the sheets go out in a lull, the boat slows, the apparent wind goes aft and the loop runs backwards. Foiler sailors bear away in gusts to turn extra wind into speed, then head up as the speed builds, keeping the apparent wind at the angle where the sails work best.

Misconceptions

  • "Sailing faster than the wind breaks conservation of energy." The boat takes energy from the true wind, which keeps blowing past it. Like a skier traversing a slope, it can move faster than the thing driving it, because it moves across the wind, not with it.
  • "The loop runs forever." It stops when the apparent wind has swung so far ahead that the forward part of the force only balances the drag.

16.2Where the loop stops: the drag angle

Every foil's force leans back from square to the flow by its : the angle whose tangent is drag ÷ lift. The rig has one, and so does whatever stops the boat sliding sideways: the keel and hull, the foils, the runners on the ice. In steady sailing, with drive just equal to drag, the apparent wind sits ahead of the course by exactly the sum of those two drag angles, β. That is where the loop stops.

The wind triangle then fixes the speed. The smaller β, the further the apparent wind can swing ahead before the loop stops, and the faster the boat goes. The best speed is with the true wind just behind the beam, at 90° + β, where boat speed ÷ wind speed = 1 ÷ sin β. A boat with β = 30° can reach at twice the wind speed; five times the wind needs β of about 11.5°. The model's iceboat, with friction-light runners and a clean rig, settles at 11° and sails at 5.2 times the wind; the AC75-style foiler at about 15° and 3.9 times.

Keelboat

about 0.6× the wind at best

The hull's wave drag grows so fast near hull speed (Module 8) that drag swamps the side force: the hull's drag angle is huge, and the loop stops with the apparent wind near 50° off the bow.

Foiling Moth

about 2× the wind

On its foils it leaves the hull's drag behind, but a sailor hanging out in the wind and a small sail give it a fairly big drag angle.

AC75-style foiler

about 4× the wind

Efficient foils, a huge righting moment and a very clean rig: the apparent wind ends up about 15° off the bow on most headings.

Iceboat

about 5× the wind

Steel runners on ice barely resist forward motion but grip sideways. Among the smallest drag angles of any sailing craft, and the fastest speed ratios.

In the wind triangle, the angle at the boat is β and the angle between the true wind and the boat wind is γ − β, where γ is the true wind angle. The sine rule gives:

V ÷ W = sin(γ − β) ÷ sin β     best at γ = 90° + β: V ÷ W = 1 ÷ sin β

On a polar this is a circle through the origin. The best VMGs are where horizontal lines touch it:

upwind VMG ÷ W = (1 − sin β) ÷ (2 sin β)     downwind VMG ÷ W = (1 + sin β) ÷ (2 sin β)

Example: β = 11.5°, sin β = 0.2: top speed 5× the wind, upwind VMG 2×, downwind VMG 3×. Upwind VMG beats the wind only when sin β < 1/3, β < 19.5°. In this ideal case the downwind VMG is always exactly one wind speed more than the upwind VMG. Real boats fall short of it because some of their drag (friction, the weight they carry) does not shrink with side force; then β grows as the sails depower, which is why a Moth's top speed is only about twice the wind.

16.3Faster than the wind, upwind and downwind

What wins races is not top speed across the wind but (Module 9): speed made good straight into, or straight away from, the wind. For a fast enough boat, both beat the wind.

Upwind, a foiler sails at about 50° to the true wind but at two to three times its speed, so the part of its speed towards the wind is still more than the wind speed. Downwind is more surprising. Running straight downwind, a boat can never beat the wind: as it approaches wind speed the apparent wind falls to zero, and so does the force. So fast boats never run. They gybe downwind at 130–150° to the true wind, fast enough that their apparent wind stays ahead of the beam, often only 10–30° off the bow. This is : sheets in tight, crew leaning out, a strong headwind on a downwind leg.

Why it happens

VMG is speed × cos(angle to the wind). A slow boat gains little by sailing at an angle. A boat that sails at four times the wind speed can sail 50° off the wind and still make 2.5 times the wind speed straight downwind. Downwind VMG beats upwind VMG because the air itself is moving downwind. In the drag angle model of 16.2 the polar is a circle whose centre lies half a wind speed downwind of its start, so its lowest point is a whole wind speed further downwind than its highest point is upwind.

On the water

On a downwind leg, a foiler or skiff sailor steers by the apparent wind: head up a little to build speed and bring the apparent wind forward, then bear away as far as the speed allows. Drop off too deep and the apparent wind goes aft, the boat slows, and on a foiler it can drop off its foils.

Misconceptions

  • "Downwind, the wind pushes you, so you can't beat it." True only for a boat sailing straight downwind. Gybing across the wind, a lift-driven boat makes good a VMG up to three times the wind speed.
  • "Upwind VMG faster than the wind means the boat points very high." Foilers point lower than keelboats, about 45–55° to the true wind. They beat the wind by going much faster, not by pointing higher.

16.4Foils: taking the hull out of the loop

A keelboat's loop is stopped by its hull. Near the wave drag climbs like a wall, so the hull's drag angle grows huge and the boat stays slower than the wind on most headings. Planing hulls get past the wall, but still drag a lot of wetted hull through the water.

What is left in the water is a few thin, efficient foils, whose drag is a small fraction of the weight they carry. The drag angle collapses, and the loop can run on to several times the wind speed.

The catch is the take-off. Below take-off speed the hull is still in the water, with its drag hump, and the loop may not have enough drive to climb over it. Sailors give it a kick: head up to a beam reach for the strongest drive, pump the sail, or wait for a gust. Once foiling, the boat can bear away and keep its speed, because now its own speed makes the apparent wind.

What you should now understand

  • Speed makes apparent wind, apparent wind makes force, force makes speed: a self-reinforcing loop.
  • The loop's brake: as speed grows the apparent wind swings forward and the force turns sideways.
  • It stops when the apparent wind is ahead of the course by the sum of the sail's and the hull's drag angles, β. Top speed ≈ wind speed ÷ sin β: five times the wind needs β ≈ 11.5°.
  • Upwind VMG beats the wind when β is under about 20°. Downwind, fast boats gybe at 130–150° with the apparent wind ahead of the beam; running dead downwind can never beat the wind.
  • Hulls stop the loop at hull speed; foils lift the hull out of the water and let it run, once the boat has taken off.
On the water

The foiler in the question makes most of its own wind. At 40-plus knots its headwind dwarfs the 12-knot breeze, so its apparent wind comes from about 15° off the bow on every leg, which is why the sails stay in tight even downwind. Its foils give it a drag angle of about 15°, so the loop runs on to about four times the wind. A cruising yacht's loop stops at hull speed, with the apparent wind still well aft, well short of the wind's own speed.
